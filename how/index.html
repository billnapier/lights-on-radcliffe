How?

What goes into putting together a 2,000+ light synchronized holiday display.

So What Goes Into This Lightshow?

First and foremost, a lot of wires—power cables, Ethernet cables, and pixel cables to name a few. For power, I use landscaping extension cords and splitters throughout the yard. All of the pixel controllers run on a dedicated, isolated Ethernet show network (with no direct internet access), while the Raspberry Pi bridges over Wi-Fi so it can stay synced to the clock and receive live song requests from viewers' phones.

Hardware

  • 2 Ethernet switches: One up on the front porch to run Ethernet to everything on the right side of the house, and a second Ethernet switch in the garage to split out the show network for the left side of the yard.
  • 3 AlphaPix 4 Classic Pixel Controllers (from HolidayCoro): One runs the house eaves, windows, and garage outline; one runs the left yard (arches, 3 left mini-trees, and the "Tune to 88.3" sign); and one runs the right driveway (3 right mini-trees plus the star on top of the Mega Tree).
  • 2 AlphaPix Flex Controllers (from HolidayCoro): One runs the 16-strand "Mega Tree" (800 pixels across 16 universes), and a second Flex Controller is configured to run AC lights (on/off/dim) for the big ground lights and yard unicorn in front of the garage.
  • 1 Raspberry Pi 4 (Model B): This used to be a much older Raspberry Pi that I just happened to have lying around, but it was super underpowered and the show audio would glitch sometimes. After upgrading to the Pi 4, I've not had any problems with it at all. It runs the nightly schedule (5:00–10:00 PM, December 1 through January 6), broadcasts the music to the FM transmitter on 88.3 FM, triggers automation scripts to turn the yard inflatables on and off with the show, and streams E1.31 pixel data across 26 universes to the controllers.

Software

Three main pieces of software power the show:

  • xLights: Open-source sequencer software. This lets me import a music track and the layout of the house and yard, and choreograph what every single light does frame by frame. It is free.
  • Falcon Player (FPP): The free show-player distribution running on the Raspberry Pi 4 that schedules and plays the synchronized sequences and audio every evening.
  • Remote Falcon: An FPP plugin that connects the show to our Pick a Song page so visitors parked outside can choose which song plays next right from their phone.

How About the Lights Themselves?

In total, the display uses over 2,000 individually addressable RGB pixel nodes (~2,200 LEDs) plus AC-controlled ground lights:

  • House outline & windows (263 pixels): Built on PVC pipe with lights zip-tied along the eaves, garage outline, and Bedroom, Kitchen, and Office window frames. HolidayCoro sells transparent clips that stay on the house year-round so the PVC sections snap up and down each season.
  • Mega Tree & Star (890 pixels): The 16-strand Mega Tree (800 pixels) is a HolidayCoro kit mounted to a vertical fence rail, topped with a 90-pixel 3-ring star.
  • 6 Mini-Trees (640 pixels): Three on each side of the yard (HolidayCoro kits that fold flat for storage). Along with the Mega Tree, these also double as singing faces during songs like The Chipmunk Song!
  • 4 Glowing Arches (96 nodes / 288 LEDs): DIY arches made from transparent PEX pipe with RGB LED strips fed through the inside (3 LEDs per addressable node) and held in shape by PVC bases.
  • "Tune to 88.3" Radio Sign (121 pixels): A custom pixel sign on the left side of the yard (dimmed down so it stays crisp and easy to read from the street at night).
  • AC Ground Lights, Unicorn & Inflatables: Traditional AC lights controlled by the AC AlphaPix Flex controller, plus inflatables automatically powered on and off by FPP at the start and end of each evening's show.
